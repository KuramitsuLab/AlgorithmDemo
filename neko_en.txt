1

I am a cat. I do not have a name yet.

I have no idea where I was born. I only remember crying "meow, meow" in a dark, damp place. There I saw a human for the first time. Later I heard that he was a "student," the cruelest kind of human. They say these students sometimes catch us, boil us and eat us. But at that time I did not know this, so I was not afraid. He picked me up in his hand, and I only felt a soft, floating feeling as I was lifted. When I settled down a little in his hand and looked at his face, that was probably the first time I ever saw a "human." I remember that I thought it was strange. A face should be covered with fur, but his was smooth, like a kettle. I have met many cats since then, but I have never met one with a face so strange. Also, the middle of his face sticks out too much. And sometimes smoke comes puffing out of the holes in it. It made me cough, and I was very troubled. Only recently did I learn that this is the tobacco that humans smoke.

I sat comfortably in the student's hand for a while. Then, suddenly, it began to move very fast. I could not tell if the student was moving or only I was, but my eyes spun wildly. I felt sick. I thought I would surely die, and then there was a thump and I saw sparks in my eyes. I remember that much. After that, I cannot remember anything, however hard I try.

When I came to my senses, the student was gone. My many brothers and sisters were gone too. Even my mother had disappeared. And the place was strangely bright, unlike the place before. I could hardly keep my eyes open. Something was wrong. I slowly crawled out, and it hurt a lot. I had been thrown from the straw into a field of bamboo grass.

With great effort I crawled out of the grass, and there was a big pond ahead. I sat in front of the pond and wondered what to do. I could not think of anything. After a while I thought that if I cried, the student might come back for me. I tried crying "meow, meow," but nobody came. Meanwhile the wind blew softly over the pond, and the sun began to set. I became very hungry. I wanted to cry, but no voice came out. There was nothing to do. I decided to walk to some place with food, and slowly began to walk around the pond on the left. It was very hard. I forced myself to crawl on, and finally I reached a place that smelled of humans. I thought that something would happen if I went in, so I crept through a hole in a broken bamboo fence into a house.

Fate is strange. If that fence had not been broken, I might have starved to death by the roadside. People say "even sharing a shade under a tree is fate," and it is true. Even now, that hole in the fence is the way I use when I visit Mike, the cat next door.

I had sneaked into the yard, but I did not know what to do next. It got dark, I was hungry, it was cold, and it began to rain. I could not wait any longer. So I walked toward the places that looked bright and warm. Thinking about it now, I was already inside the house. There I got another chance to see humans other than that student. The first one I met was Osan, the maid. She was even rougher than the student. As soon as she saw me, she grabbed my neck and threw me outside. I thought, "This is bad," closed my eyes, and left it to fate. But I could not stand the hunger and the cold. When Osan was not looking, I climbed into the kitchen again. Soon I was thrown out again. I remember that I was thrown out and climbed back in, climbed in and was thrown out, four or five times. Then I came to hate Osan very much. Some time ago I stole a fish from her, and finally I felt better. Just as she was about to throw me out for the last time, the master of the house came out and asked, "What is all this noise?" The maid held me up by the neck, showed me to him, and said, "This stray kitten keeps coming into the kitchen, no matter how many times I throw it out." The master twisted the black hairs under his nose and looked at my face for a while. Then he said, "Well, let it stay here," and went back inside. He seemed to be a man who did not talk much. The maid angrily threw me down in the kitchen. And so I decided to make this house my home.

My master rarely meets me. They say he is a teacher. After he comes home from school, he stays in his study all day and hardly ever comes out. The people in the house think he works very hard. He also acts as if he works hard. But he is not really as hard-working as they say. Sometimes I quietly look into his study, and he is often taking a nap. Sometimes he drools on the book he was reading. He has a weak stomach, and his skin is pale yellow, with no energy. Even so, he eats a lot. After eating a lot, he takes stomach medicine. After taking it, he opens a book. After reading two or three pages, he becomes sleepy. He drools on the book. This is what he does every night. I am only a cat, but sometimes I think: being a teacher must be very easy. If you are born a human, you should become a teacher. If you can do the job while sleeping so much, even a cat could do it. But the master says that nothing is harder than being a teacher, and he complains about it every time a friend comes.

When I first came to live in this house, I was very unpopular with everyone except the master. Wherever I went, I was pushed away, and nobody would play with me. You can tell how unwanted I was from the fact that, even now, nobody has given me a name. I had no choice, so I tried to stay as close as I could to the master, who had let me in. In the morning, when he reads the newspaper, I always sit on his lap. When he takes a nap, I always sit on his back. It is not because I like him very much. There was simply nobody else to be with. Later, after trying many places, I decided on the rice container in the morning, the heated table at night, and the porch on sunny afternoons. But the most comfortable place is the bed of the children of this house, at night. I creep into it and sleep with them. The children are five and three years old, and at night the two sleep in one bed in one room. I always find a little space between them and somehow squeeze in. But if one of the children wakes up, it is terrible. The children, especially the younger one, are the troublemakers. They cry loudly in the middle of the night, "The cat came! The cat came!" Then the master, with his weak stomach and nervous nature, always wakes up and rushes in from the next room. Just the other day he hit my bottom hard with a ruler.

The more I live with humans and watch them, the more I must say that they are selfish. This is especially true of the children I sometimes sleep with. When they feel like it, they turn me upside down, put a bag over my head, throw me, or push me into the stove. But if I do the slightest thing back, the whole family chases me and punishes me. The other day I sharpened my claws a little on the straw mat, and the wife got very angry. Since then she does not easily let me into the living room. They do not care at all if I shiver on the wooden floor of the kitchen. My respected friend White, who lives across the street, says every time we meet that nothing is as heartless as humans. White recently gave birth to four beautiful kittens. But the student at that house took them to the pond behind the house on the third day and threw all four away. White cried as she told me the whole story. She said that if we cats want to keep our parent-child love and live happy family lives, we must fight humans and destroy them. I think this is quite right. Also, Mike next door is very angry that humans do not understand the idea of ownership. Among us cats, whoever finds something first, even a fish head or a fish belly, has the right to eat it. If another cat does not follow this rule, it is fine to use force. But humans do not seem to have this idea at all, and they always steal the good food we find. They use their strength to take what we have every right to eat, and they do not care. White lives in a soldier's house, and Mike's master is a lawyer. Since I live in a teacher's house, I am more relaxed about such things than they are. I only want to get through each day somehow. Humans cannot stay on top forever. Well, I should wait patiently for the time of the cats.

Speaking of selfishness, let me tell you a story of how my master failed because of his own selfishness. This master is not really good at anything, but he likes to try everything. He writes haiku and sends them to a magazine. He sends modern poems to another one. He writes English full of mistakes. Sometimes he is crazy about archery, or learns to sing old songs, or plays the violin with loud noises. Sadly, none of them has turned out well. Still, once he starts something, he is very serious about it, even though he has a weak stomach. He sings in the toilet, and the neighbors have nicknamed him "Professor Toilet." He does not care at all and keeps singing the same song, "I am Taira no Munemori." People burst out laughing, saying, "There goes Munemori." One day, about a month after I came to live here, on payday, the master came home in a hurry with a big package. I wondered what he had bought. It was watercolor paints, brushes and Whatman paper. He seemed to have decided to stop the singing and haiku and take up painting. Sure enough, from the next day, for a while, he painted every day in his study without even taking a nap. But when I saw what he painted, nobody could tell what it was. He probably thought it was not good either. One day a friend of his, who studies beauty and art, came, and I heard the following talk.

"It is hard to paint well," said the master. "When I look at other people's pictures, it seems easy. But when I take up a brush myself, I find it hard all over again." This was the master's honest confession. It is surely true. His friend looked at the master through his gold-rimmed glasses and said, "Of course you cannot paint well from the start. And you cannot paint a picture just from imagining the inside of a room. The great Italian master Andrea del Sarto once said, 'If you want to paint, copy nature itself. In the sky there are stars. On the earth there is dew. For flying there are birds. For running there are animals. In the pond there are goldfish. On the dead tree there is a winter crow. Nature is one great living picture.' So, if you want to paint real pictures, you should draw from life."

"Oh, did Andrea del Sarto say that? I did not know. That makes sense. You are quite right," said the master, deeply impressed. Behind the gold rims, I saw a mocking smile.

The next day, as usual, I was napping happily on the porch. The master came out of his study, which was not usual, and was busy doing something behind me. I woke up and opened my eyes a little. He was completely absorbed in following Andrea del Sarto. I could not help laughing at the sight. Because his friend had teased him, he was now beginning to draw me. I had already slept enough. I badly wanted to yawn. But the master was working so hard with his brush that I felt sorry for him and stayed still. He had drawn my outline and was now coloring the face. I must confess something. As a cat, I am not very good-looking. I do not think my back, my fur or my face is better than other cats'. But even though I am ugly, I cannot believe that I look like the strange figure the master was drawing. First, the color was wrong. I have light gray fur with a yellow tint, like a Persian cat, and black spots like lacquer. Anyone would agree on that. But the master's color was neither yellow nor black, neither gray nor brown, and not a mix of them either. It was simply "a color," and there was no other way to describe it. Even stranger, there were no eyes. Of course, I was sleeping in his picture, so that is natural. But since there was nothing like an eye, nobody could tell whether it was a blind cat or a sleeping cat. I thought quietly, "Even Andrea del Sarto could do nothing with this." But I had to admire his effort. I wanted to stay still if I could, but I needed to pee. The muscles in my body were itching. I could not wait even a minute, so I apologized in my heart, stretched my front legs out, pushed my head down low, and gave a big yawn. Once that happened, there was no use being quiet. I had already ruined the master's plan, so I slowly crawled away to go and do my business in the back. Then the master, in a voice full of disappointment and anger, shouted from the room, "You fool!" This master always says "fool" when he scolds someone. He knows no other bad word, so it cannot be helped. But he does not know how much I had been holding still for him, and I think it is rude to call me a fool for no reason. If he were kind to me even a little when I sit on his back, I would accept this abuse. But he has never done anything pleasant for me, and he calls me a fool just because I went to pee. How cruel! Humans are all proud of their own power and have become too big for themselves. If something a little stronger than humans does not appear and teach them a lesson, there is no telling how far they will go.

I can accept this much selfishness. But I once heard a story about human wrongdoing that was many times sadder than this.

Behind my house there is a tea garden about ten tsubo in size. It is not large, but it is neat and pleasant, and the sun shines on it. When the children are too noisy for me to nap, or when I am too bored and my stomach feels bad, I always go there to refresh my spirit. One calm, warm day in late autumn, around two o'clock, after a good nap following lunch, I went to the tea garden for some exercise. I was smelling the tea plants one by one and had reached the cedar hedge on the west side, when I saw a big cat lying on a crushed dry chrysanthemum, fast asleep. He did not seem to notice me coming, or maybe he noticed and did not care. He lay stretched out, snoring loudly. I secretly marveled at his courage, sleeping so calmly in someone else's yard. He was a pure black cat. The sun, just past noon, poured clear light on his coat, and it seemed as if an invisible flame was burning among the shining soft hairs. He had the great body of a king among cats. He was surely twice my size. I forgot everything, full of admiration and curiosity, and stood in front of him, staring. A gentle autumn breeze moved the branches of a paulownia tree above the cedar hedge, and two or three leaves fell into the dry chrysanthemums. The king suddenly opened his round eyes wide. I still remember it. His eyes shone far more beautifully than amber, which humans treasure. He did not move. With a light that seemed to shoot out from deep in his eyes, he looked at my small forehead and said, "Who the hell are you?" I thought his words were rather rough for a king, but there was a power in his voice that could scare even a dog, so I felt quite afraid. But I thought it would be dangerous not to greet him, so I answered as calmly as I could, "I am a cat. I do not have a name yet." But my heart was beating much harder than usual.

He said in a very scornful voice, "What? A cat? Don't make me laugh. Where do you live, anyway?" He was very rude. "I live in the teacher's house here," I said. "I thought so. You are pretty skinny," he said, boasting like a king. From his way of talking, I could tell he was not from a good family. But he was fat and shiny, so he seemed to eat well and live richly. I had to ask, "And who are you?" "I'm Kuro, from the rickshaw man's house," he said proudly. Kuro, the rickshaw man's cat, is a violent cat known to everyone around here. But because he is only a rickshaw man's cat, he is strong but has no education, so nobody wants to be friends with him. Everyone keeps away from him. When I heard his name, I felt a little uneasy, but at the same time I felt a little contempt. I decided to test how ignorant he was, and asked the following.

"Which is greater, a rickshaw man or a teacher?"

"The rickshaw man is stronger, of course. Look at your master. He is nothing but skin and bones."

"You are strong too, being a rickshaw man's cat. It seems you can eat good food at a rickshaw man's house."

"Hah, I never have trouble finding food, wherever I go. You should not just walk around the tea garden all day. Follow me for a while. In less than a month you will be so fat that no one will know you."

"Maybe I will ask you some other time. But it seems that a teacher lives in a bigger house than a rickshaw man."

"Stupid! No matter how big a house is, it does not fill your stomach."

He was very angry, twitching his ears, which looked like cut bamboo, and walked away roughly. This was how I became acquainted with Kuro, the rickshaw man's cat.

After that, I met Kuro often. Every time we met, he bragged in a way that suited a rickshaw man's cat. The story about human wrongdoing that I mentioned before was also something I heard from Kuro.

One day, as usual, Kuro and I were lying in the warm tea garden, chatting about many things. He repeated his usual bragging as if it were new, and then asked me, "How many rats have you caught so far?" I thought my knowledge was much better than Kuro's, but I knew I could never compare with him in strength and courage. Still, when I heard the question, I felt a bit embarrassed. But the truth is the truth, and I could not lie. I answered, "Actually, I have wanted to catch one, but I have not caught any yet." Kuro shook the long whiskers sticking out from his nose and laughed a lot. Kuro brags so much because something is missing in him. If you listen carefully to his boasting, purring in your throat as if impressed, he is a very easy cat to handle. I had learned this soon after I met him. So this time too, I decided that defending myself would only make things worse. It would be better to let him talk about his own glories and avoid trouble. So I quietly said, "You are older, so you must have caught many." As I expected, he charged at the weak spot in the wall. "Not so many, but maybe thirty or forty," he answered proudly. He went on, "I can take on a hundred or two hundred rats alone, anytime, but a weasel is too much for me. I once had a terrible time with a weasel." "Oh, really," I said, nodding.

Kuro blinked his big eyes and said, "It was last year, at the big spring cleaning. My master was crawling under the floor of the porch with a bag of lime, and a big weasel jumped out, surprised." "Hmm," I said, pretending to be impressed. "A weasel is just a little bigger than a rat. I thought, 'You rascal!' and chased it all the way into the ditch." "That was well done," I cheered. "But then, right at the last moment, the thing let out a stinky fart! It smelled so bad, and ever since, I feel sick whenever I see a weasel." At this point, as if he could still smell last year's stink, he raised his front paw and rubbed his nose two or three times. I felt a little sorry for him. I wanted to cheer him up, so I said, "But rats must be terrified when you glare at them. You are such a master rat catcher and eat so many rats that you are fat and shiny." This question, meant to please Kuro, strangely had the opposite effect. He sighed deeply and said, "When I think about it, it is all pointless. No matter how hard I work catching rats, there is nobody in the world as shameless as humans. They take all the rats I catch and bring them to the police box. The police box does not know who caught them, so they give five sen each time. My master has already made about one yen fifty sen thanks to me, but he has never given me a decent meal. Hey, humans are polite thieves." Even uneducated Kuro seemed to understand this much, and he was very angry, his back hair standing up. I felt a little scared, so I somehow got away from the place and went home. From that time, I decided never to catch rats. But I did not become Kuro's follower and go hunting for good food other than rats either. I would rather sleep than eat good food. It seems that if a cat lives in a teacher's house, it becomes like a teacher. I should be careful, or I might get a weak stomach.

Speaking of teachers, my master, too, seems to have given up on watercolors by now, and on the first of December he wrote this in his diary.

Today, at a party, I met Mr. So-and-so for the first time. They say he has lived a wild life, and indeed he looks like a man of the world. Men like him are loved by women, so it would be better to say that he was forced to live wildly rather than that he chose it. His wife is a geisha, they say. How I envy him! Most people who speak badly of wild men are those who lack the qualification to be wild. And among those who think of themselves as wild men, there are many who also lack the qualification. They are not forced to do it, but they push themselves to do it. It is just like my watercolors, which I will never finish. Even so, they think of themselves as men of the world and feel satisfied. If drinking sake at restaurants and going to teahouses makes a man a man of the world, then I too could become a fine watercolor painter. Just as it is better for me not to paint, a country fellow with no manners is far better than a foolish man of the world.

I cannot quite agree with his idea about men of the world. Also, envying a geisha wife is a foolish thing for a teacher to say. But his judgment about his own watercolors is correct. In this way, the master knows himself well, yet his vanity does not go away easily. Two days later, on December 4, he wrote this in his diary.

Last night I dreamed that I had painted a watercolor, thought it was hopeless, and thrown it aside, and that somebody had put it in a fine frame and hung it above the door. When I saw it in the frame, I thought I had suddenly become good. I was very happy. I was looking at it alone, thinking, "This is a fine picture," when morning came, I woke up, and with the morning sun it became clear that it was bad, as before.

It seems that the master is carrying his love of watercolors even into his dreams. With this, he will never become a watercolor painter, nor a "man of the world" as he calls it.

The day after the master dreamed of his watercolor, the gold-rimmed-glasses scholar of beauty came to see him after a long time. As soon as he sat down, he asked, "How is the painting?" The master answered calmly, "I have been trying to draw from life, following your advice. Indeed, when I draw from life, I begin to notice the shapes of things and small changes in color that I never noticed before. I think the West became so advanced because they have always insisted on drawing from life. Andrea del Sarto was truly great." He said nothing about his diary and admired Andrea del Sarto again. The scholar laughed, scratched his head, and said, "Actually, that was nonsense." "What was?" said the master, who still did not realize he had been tricked. "What do you mean, what? The Andrea del Sarto you admire so much. I made that story up. I never thought you would believe it so seriously. Ha ha ha!" He was delighted. Listening to this from the porch, I could not help wondering what the master would write in his diary today. This scholar's only pleasure is to spread such careless lies and trick people. Without thinking at all about how the Andrea del Sarto trick had hurt the master's feelings, he proudly went on to say the following.

"You know, when I joke, people often take it seriously, and the funny result amuses me. The other day I told a student that Nicholas Nickleby had advised Gibbon to stop writing his great History of the French Revolution in French and to publish it in English. That student had a very good memory, and he repeated exactly what I told him at a talk of the Japanese Literature Society. It was so funny. About a hundred people were in the audience, and they all listened eagerly. And I have another funny story. The other day, at a gathering with some writers, someone mentioned Harrison's historical novel Theophano. I said that it was the best of all historical novels, and that the scene where the heroine dies is so chilling. Then a gentleman sitting across from me, one of those who never say 'I don't know,' said, 'Yes, yes, that part is truly a fine piece of writing.' So I learned that he, like me, had not read the book."

The master, with his nervous stomach, opened his eyes wide and asked, "If you tell such lies, what will you do if the other person has read it?" He seemed to feel that tricking people is fine, but being found out would be a problem. The scholar was not troubled at all. "Then I will just say I mixed it up with another book," he said, and laughed out loud. This scholar wears gold-rimmed glasses, but his nature is somewhat like Kuro's. The master silently blew out smoke in rings, looking as if he had no such courage. The scholar looked at him as if to say, "That is why you cannot paint," and said, "But joking aside, painting really is hard. They say Leonardo da Vinci taught his students to copy the stains on temple walls. Indeed, if you go into the toilet and look at a leaking wall, you will find that nice patterns have formed naturally. Try drawing them from life. You will surely get something interesting." "You are tricking me again, aren't you?" "No, this one is true. Is it not a clever saying? It sounds just like something da Vinci would say." "It is certainly clever," said the master, half giving in. But it seems he has not yet tried drawing in the toilet.

After that, Kuro the rickshaw man's cat became lame. His shiny fur slowly lost its color and began to fall out. His eyes, which I once said were more beautiful than amber, were full of eye discharge. What I noticed most was that he had lost his spirit and his body had become weak. On the last day I met him in the tea garden, I asked how he was, and he said, "I have had enough of the weasel's last fart and the fish seller's carrying pole."

The red leaves between the red pines, which looked like a few steps of crimson, fell away like an old dream. The red and white camellias near the stone water basin dropped their petals one after another and have now all fallen. The winter sun sets early on the south-facing porch, three and a half ken wide. Now that the cold wind blows almost every day, I feel my nap time has become shorter.

The master goes to school every day. When he comes home, he shuts himself in his study. When people visit, he says how much he hates being a teacher. He rarely paints now. He also stopped taking stomach medicine, saying it does not work. The children, to their credit, go to kindergarten without missing a day. When they come home, they sing songs, bounce a ball, and sometimes hang me by the tail.

I do not eat fine food, so I do not get fat, but I am fairly healthy and spend my days without becoming lame. I never catch rats. I still dislike Osan. They have not given me a name yet, but there is no end to wanting things, so I plan to live out my life as a nameless cat in this teacher's house.
